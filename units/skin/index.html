    Skin: Light Under the Surface

    Skin glows because light travels under it before it comes back out. This unit builds the techniques games use for that, from a one-line wrap to diffusion profiles, pre-integration and light through ears, plus the two-lobe specular on top.

    Builds on Inside a PBR Shader. Engine notes cover Unity HDRP, Unity URP and Unreal.

    Why skin is not Lambert

    A Lambert surface reflects light from the exact point where it arrives (the diffuse term). Skin does not. Most light enters it, scatters around in the layers underneath, and leaves a few millimetres away. That softens every shadow edge and gives the transition from lit to unlit a warm red band, because red light travels much farther through tissue than green or blue before it is absorbed:

    The numbers here are illustrative, of the order engines use for skin. What matters is the ratio: red reaches several times deeper than blue. Every technique in this unit is a way to spread the lighting sideways by those distances, cheaply.

    The cheapest trick: wrap lighting

    The simplest approximation lets the light "wrap" past the point where N·L reaches zero, as if it scattered a little around the curve. Shift the cosine by w and divide by (1 + w) so a surface facing the light stays at the same brightness (Lambert is w = 0):

    Using a larger w for red than for blue gives the warm terminator. Wrap lighting is a single line and runs on any platform, which makes it the usual choice for mobile, stylised characters and URP. It ignores the size of the object, though: the same w softens a nose and a forehead equally, which the next chapters fix.

    The diffusion profile

    Shine a thin laser into skin and the glow around the entry point fades with distance. That falloff is the R(r). For human skin it is well described by a sum of a few Gaussians of different widths, one weight per colour channel (the fit from NVIDIA's skin research). A wide red Gaussian is what carries red light far:

    All skin techniques use some form of this profile: blur the diffuse lighting by R. The engines expose it as a scattering distance per colour (HDRP) or a subsurface profile asset (Unreal), from which they build the kernel.

    Blurring in screen space

    The most common real-time method renders the diffuse lighting of skin into its own buffer (a render target), then blurs it with the profile as a kernel, in screen space. The kernel must cover the same number of millimetres at every distance, so its size in pixels shrinks as the face moves away:

    The blur also reads the depth buffer and skips samples that lie on a different surface, otherwise the face would bleed into the background. Specular light is kept out of the blur: it reflects at the surface and stays sharp.

    Set the material type to Subsurface Scattering and assign a Diffusion Profile asset. The profile's Scattering Distance colour and World Scale are the r of this chapter; up to 15 profiles are active per frame, listed in the HDRP global settings.

    URP has no screen-space SSS. Use wrap lighting or the pre-integrated method of the next chapter in Shader Graph, plus the transmission trick for ears.

    Use the Subsurface Profile shading model and a Subsurface Profile asset (scatter radius, falloff colour, dual specular parameters). The screen-space pass is controlled with r.SSS.* settings.

    Pre-integrated skin

    A cheaper method avoids the extra pass. On a curved surface, the scattered light around a point comes from neighbours that face the light at slightly different angles. If the surface is a sphere of radius r, that blur can be computed once, for every N·L and every radius, and stored in a small lookup texture. The shader then reads it with N·L and the local 1/r (from the change of the normal between pixels, or a baked curvature map):

    With the profile as measured, the effect is limited to very small radii: nostril rims, ear edges, fine wrinkles. Productions usually widen the profile (the scale slider) so that noses, lips and eyelids also get a soft red terminator, while cheeks and forehead stay close to Lambert. The lookup texture is computed exactly as the widget does it, once, offline.

    Light through ears

    Backlit ears and nostrils glow red because light passes all the way through thin tissue. Shaders model it separately from the surface scattering: a stores how thick the object is at each texel (baked by casting rays inward), and the light that makes it through falls off exponentially with thickness, per colour, like Beer–Lambert:

    The light used for transmission comes from behind, so the shader uses max(0, −N·L) (often wrapped as well). HDRP can also take the thickness from the shadow map instead of a texture, which handles animated characters better.

    Skin specular: two lobes

    The surface of skin is covered in a thin oily layer over a rougher base, so its highlight is a sharp, faint lobe on top of a broad one. Engines model it as two GGX lobes with different roughness, mixed. The reflectance at normal incidence is low, F0 ≈ 0.028 (refractive index about 1.4), and a cavity map darkens specular in pores and wrinkles:

    Pore detail lives in a tiling micro-normal map blended over the main normal map (blending normals), faded with distance like any detail map.
