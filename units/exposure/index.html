    Sun to Screen

    A sunlit wall is ten thousand times brighter than the same wall at night, and your monitor shows about a thousand to one. Exposure and tonemapping squeeze one into the other. Every formula on the way is live.

    Units of light, EV100 and the physical camera, auto exposure, tonemapping curves and display encoding, with notes for Unity HDRP, Unity URP and Unreal.

    Too much range

    Every pixel in the picture below stores a : how bright that spot looks, in candela per square metre (cd/m², also called nits), the same quantity a light meter reads. About 2 in the shadow under the table, about 20 on the wall, about 7 000 in the sky and 50 000 on the lamp bulb. These are HDR values: nothing caps them at 1.

    Photographers count brightness differences in . One stop is a factor of two. Because brightness spans such huge ratios, counting doublings is much handier than writing the ratio itself:

    The room spans about fifteen stops. An ordinary monitor shows roughly ten, and the image travels to it as 8-bit numbers from 0 to 255. Something has to decide which slice of the scene ends up visible. In a camera that is ; in a renderer it is one multiplier on every pixel, followed by a curve that bends the highlights instead of cutting them off.

    Four units of light

    Light can be measured at four points on its way from a lamp to your eye, and lighting tools use all four. Follow one bulb through them, one formula at a time.

    1. (lm): everything the bulb emits. The number printed on the box: a 60 W incandescent bulb gives about 800 lm, in all directions together.

    2. (cd): light per direction. Spread the lumens evenly over every direction. "Every direction" is a full sphere, which measures 4π (the 3D version of "a full circle is 2π radians"):

    3. (lx): light arriving on a surface. The light in one direction spreads out as it travels. At twice the distance, it covers twice the width and twice the height, four times the area: a quarter of the light per square metre. That is the inverse-square law. Tilting the surface spreads the same light over a longer patch, which multiplies by cos θ, the same factor as in Lambert's law:

    4. (cd/m²): how bright the surface looks. A surface reflects the fraction ρ of the light it receives, its . A matte surface scatters that light evenly over the half-sphere above it, which divides by π:

    The cone that covers the lit patch at distance d covers twice the height at 2d, and twice the depth into the screen too: four times the area for the same light, so a quarter of the lux.

    HDRP lights are physical by default. Point and spot lights take Lumen, Candela, Lux (at a distance) or EV100; directional lights take Lux; area lights also take Nits. A spot light's lumens are spread over its cone only when Reflector is enabled, otherwise over the full sphere.

    Keep world units at 1 unit = 1 m. A room modeled in centimeters at 100× scale gets 10 000× less light from the same lumen value.

    URP light intensity is a unitless multiplier, not a photometric unit. You can still think in this chain, but you pick a scale yourself, for example "intensity 1 = sun", and stay consistent across scenes. URP point lights use inverse-square falloff with a smooth fade to the range, so distance behaves physically even though the units do not.

    Unreal directional lights are in lux. Point, spot and rect lights choose their unit per light: Candelas, Lumens or Unitless (the legacy scale). Spot lights in lumens keep the same total light when you change the cone, so a narrower cone gets brighter. Use inverse squared falloff for physical behaviour, and keep 1 unit = 1 cm in mind when you read distances.

    EV100 and the camera

    A camera has three controls for how much light reaches the image. The is the size of the hole, written as an f-number N: smaller numbers are bigger holes. The time t is how long the hole stays open. The S is how strongly the sensor amplifies what it collects.

    Each control can be counted in stops, and the three stop counts add up to one number, the . The f-number goes in squared because the hole's area grows with the square of its width:

    A higher EV100 means a darker image. EV100 describes how bright the scene is: a brighter scene needs a higher number to look the same. Settings with the same EV100 produce the same brightness and differ only in side effects (depth of field, motion blur, noise), which a renderer fakes separately. That is why engines expose a single EV100 value.

    From EV to a multiplier

    A shader cannot use "f/8 at 1/125". It needs one float that turns a luminance into a value where 1.0 means "as bright as the sensor can record". Frostbite, Unity HDRP and others use this conversion, from the ISO standard for :

    The 1.2 is a fixed calibration constant (78 / (0.65 × 100): lens losses and some headroom from the standard). Write it once and forget it.

    A light meter works the other way round. It measures the scene's average luminance and assumes that average should look like a mid tone. That gives the , the formula auto exposure uses:

    Put both together and the metered average always lands at 1 / (1.2 · 8) ≈ 0.104 of the clipping point, 3.3 stops below it. That headroom is deliberate: room for highlights before anything clips. It is also why a correctly exposed linear buffer looks dark when you view it raw.

    PrecisionA 16-bit float (half) render target tops out at 65 504. Raw sunlit luminances in half precision can overflow. Engines therefore multiply by last frame's exposure before writing lighting to the buffer: .

    HDRP's ColorUtils class converts with ConvertEV100ToExposure(ev100) = 1 / (1.2 · 2^ev100) and pre-exposes lighting buffers with the previous frame's value. In C# and Shader Graph you can read the current exposure (Exposure node), which you need for emissive values that should not change with exposure.

    URP has no exposure value in the shading path. The equivalent is the Post Exposure parameter on the Color Adjustments override, measured in EV, applied before tonemapping. If you author lights in physical-ish values, put the conversion in your own scale and keep post exposure near 0.

    Unreal stores exposure as EV100 when Extend default luminance range in Auto Exposure settings is enabled in project settings (the default for new projects). Its Manual metering mode with Apply Physical Camera Exposure derives EV100 from the camera's aperture, shutter speed and ISO, the same formula as chapter 4.

    Auto exposure

    , also called eye adaptation, picks EV100 each frame from what the camera sees, in three steps.

    1. Measure. Average the frame's luminance. A plain average is ruined by a few very bright pixels: one sun-lit pixel can outweigh a thousand dark ones. Averaging the logarithm instead (the ) counts every pixel by its stops, not its raw value. Try it with a frame where a few pixels see the sky:

    Many engines go one step further and build a of log luminance, then ignore the darkest and brightest few percent.

    2. Target. Turn the measured average into EV100 with the meter equation, subtract an (positive brightens) and clamp to a minimum and maximum.

    3. Adapt. Move the current EV toward the target a little each frame. Using an exponential approach makes the speed independent of the frame rate:

    Top right: histogram of log₂ luminance. Shaded ends are ignored by histogram metering. Bottom: target EV (dashed) and adapted EV (solid) over the last few seconds.

    Add an Exposure override to a Volume. Modes: Fixed, Automatic, Automatic Histogram, Curve Mapping and Physical Camera. Automatic modes have Limit Min/Max in EV100, Compensation, a Metering Mode (average, spot, center-weighted, mask) and Adaptation with separate dark-to-light and light-to-dark speeds. Histogram mode exposes the percentage of pixels to ignore at each end.

    URP has no built-in auto exposure. Teams either keep a fixed exposure per area (set by Post Exposure in volumes, blended at area borders) or write a custom pass that reduces log luminance with a compute shader or mip chain and feeds the result back the next frame.

    The Post Process Volume Exposure section offers Auto Exposure Histogram, Auto Exposure Basic and Manual. Set Min EV100 and Max EV100 equal to lock exposure while you light. Speed Up and Speed Down control adaptation, Low/High Percent trim the histogram. Use Visualize HDR in the viewport to see the histogram live.

    Tonemapping curves

    After exposure, most values sit around 0.1, but highlights still reach 10, 100 or more. Cutting them at 1.0 gives hard, flat white. A maps every value from 0 to infinity into 0 to 1: nearly straight for the mid tones, bending gently for the highlights, the way film did.

    • Reinhard, x / (1 + x): the simplest curve. It never reaches 1, so highlights look grey.
    • Hable (the Uncharted 2 filmic curve): a small toe for the blacks, a long shoulder and a of 11.2.
    • ACES fit (Narkowicz 2016): a cheap formula that imitates the ACES reference tonemapper. More contrast than Hable.

    The graph's x axis is exposed value on a log scale, in stops around middle grey (0.18). Click a curve's value tag to compare, or animate x:

    Applied to red, green and blue separately, every curve also shifts colour in the highlights: the brightest channel flattens first, so orange fire turns yellow. Newer operators such as AgX (Blender's default since 4.0) and ACES 2 work in another colour space to control that.

    LabFour curvesThe x axis is stops around middle grey. Hover to read values.

    The strip is a ramp from −6 to +6 stops, colored orange so you can see the hue shift, run through the selected curve.

    // exposed linear value in, display linear out
float Reinhard(float x) { return x / (1 + x); }

float AcesFit(float x) {          // Narkowicz 2016
  x *= 0.6;
  return saturate((x*(2.51*x + 0.03)) /
                  (x*(2.43*x + 0.59) + 0.14));
}

float H(float x) {                // Hable 2010
  const float A=.15,B=.50,C=.10,D=.20,E=.02,F=.30;
  return ((x*(A*x+C*B)+D*E)/(x*(A*x+B)+D*F)) - E/F;
}
float Hable(float x) { return H(2*x) / H(11.2); }

    The Tonemapping override has None, Neutral, ACES, Custom (toe, shoulder, gamma controls) and External (your own LUT). With HDR output enabled, HDRP tonemaps for the display's peak brightness instead.

    The Tonemapping override offers Neutral and ACES. Use HDR grading mode in the URP asset so grading happens before the curve on the full range.

    Unreal's default tonemapper is a filmic curve fitted to ACES, tunable with Slope, Toe, Shoulder, Black Clip and White Clip in the Post Process Volume's Film section. Leave them at default until exposure is right; the curve is not a substitute for exposure.

    Encoding for the display

    The tonemapper outputs values from 0 to 1 that are still linear: twice the number, twice the light. Before they become 8-bit numbers, they are encoded with the curve, explained in Linear and sRGB. The reason is the eye: it notices small steps in dark tones much more than in bright ones, and 8 bits only give 256 steps. Count how many of them land in the dark quarter of the range, with and without the curve:

    LabWhere the codes goA ramp across the darkest quarter of display range, quantized two ways.

    8 bits is what a normal SDR display gets. Lower bit counts exaggerate the effect so it is easy to see.

    HDR displaysAn HDR10 display replaces sRGB with the PQ curve, which encodes absolute luminance up to 10 000 nits in 10 bits. The tonemapper then targets the display's peak (often 600–1 000 nits) and a "paper white" around 100–250 nits for UI and mid tones, instead of 1.0.

    A lighting workflow

    The theory turns into a short checklist. The order matters: each step assumes the one before it is settled.

    1. Fix the scale. 1 unit = 1 meter in Unity, 1 cm in Unreal. Check a door is about 2 m tall.
    2. Lock exposure. Turn auto exposure off and set a fixed EV100 that matches the situation: 15 for sun, 12 to 13 for overcast, 7 to 9 for a lit interior. You are now lighting for a camera, like a cinematographer.
    3. Set the dominant light first in physical units: the sun in lux, the sky so that a white card in shade reads believable, then interior lights from real lumen values.
    4. Check albedo. Physical lights only produce physical results with plausible albedo, roughly 0.04 to 0.9 in linear terms. A pure white 1.0 wall reflects all light and looks like it glows.
    5. Turn auto exposure back on with tight limits, a few stops either side of your fixed value, so the camera can adapt but artists stay in control.
    6. Grade last. Choose the tonemapper and color grade after exposure, never to fix it.

    Build it in Unity HDRP

    In HDRP, exposure and tonemapping are Volume overrides and lights already use physical units, so this unit maps almost one to one onto settings. The script blends exposure between two values, for example when the player walks indoors. Written for Unity 6 with HDRP 17; Unity 2022 LTS works the same apart from a few renamed menu entries.

    Engine settingsVolume overrides: Exposure and Tonemapping; light units on every light.
    Shader GraphThe Exposure and Emission nodes for emissive surfaces.
    C# scriptExposureBlend.cs: blends a fixed EV100 from C#.
    1. Lights in physical units: Directional Light in Lux (sun 100 000, overcast 10 000), point and spot lights in Lumen or Candela, emissive materials in Nits. Use the Physically Based Sky or an HDRI with a calibrated Exposure value.
    2. Add a Global Volume with a profile and Add Override > Exposure. While lighting, set Mode to Fixed and Fixed Exposure to the EV100 of the scene (sunny day 14–15, interior 7–9): that is lock exposure from the workflow.
    3. For gameplay switch to Automatic Histogram: Limit Min/Max are the EV range, Compensation shifts the target, Adaptation Speed Dark to Light / Light to Dark are the k of auto exposure. The histogram ignores the brightest and darkest percent of pixels.
    4. Add Override > Tonemapping: ACES for a filmic look, Neutral for minimal hue shifts, Custom for your own curve parameters.
    5. Emissive surfaces in Shader Graph: an Emission node with Intensity Unit Nits and Exposure Weight 1 behaves like a real light source; 0 keeps a constant brightness on screen (UI-like). The Exposure node gives the current multiplier for your own maths.
    6. Add ExposureBlend.cs, assign the Volume, and drive Interior from a trigger or a script.
    using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.Rendering;
using UnityEngine.Rendering.HighDefinition;

// Blends a fixed exposure between an outdoor and an indoor EV100 (0 = outdoors, 1 = indoors).
[ExecuteAlways]
public class ExposureBlend : MonoBehaviour
{
    public Volume volume;
    [Range(0f, 1f)] public float interior;
    public float outdoorEV100 = 14.5f;
    public float indoorEV100 = 8f;

    void Update()
    {
        if (volume == null) return;
        // In Play mode, .profile is a runtime copy; in edit mode, change the asset itself.
        VolumeProfile profile = Application.isPlaying ? volume.profile : volume.sharedProfile;
        if (profile == null || !profile.TryGet(out Exposure exposure)) return;

        exposure.mode.overrideState = true;
        exposure.mode.value = ExposureMode.Fixed;
        exposure.fixedExposure.overrideState = true;
        exposure.fixedExposure.value = Mathf.Lerp(outdoorEV100, indoorEV100, interior);
    }

    // Screen value multiplier for an EV100, the formula from the unit.
    public static float Multiplier(float ev100) => 1f / (1.2f * Mathf.Pow(2f, ev100));
}

    Blending EV100 linearly is right: EV is already logarithmic, so equal steps look like equal changes in brightness. The Rendering Debugger's Lighting > Exposure modes show the metering histogram and the current EV over the frame.

    URP has no physical exposure: lights are unitless and there is no auto exposure override. Tonemapping (ACES, Neutral) is a Volume override under Post-processing. For eye adaptation, a custom renderer feature that computes the average luminance with a compute shader (a reduction, see Compute Shaders) is the usual route.

    Post Process Volume > Exposure: Metering Mode Auto Exposure Histogram or Manual (with Apply Physical Camera Exposure), Min/Max EV100, Exposure Compensation, Speed Up/Down. Lights in lux, candela or lumens; Extend default luminance range in the project settings enables the EV100 scale.
